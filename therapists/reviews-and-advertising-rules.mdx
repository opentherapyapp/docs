## Why reviews here look different

Section 133 of the *Health Practitioner Regulation National Law* prohibits using testimonials to advertise a regulated health service. A client writing whatever they like on a third-party site is their business. A review Open Therapy chooses to publish on your profile is **our advertising of your service** — and yours.

The line the law draws inside that is *clinical* content. So a review may describe the **service**, but not the **treatment**.

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| Booking, waiting times, availability, punctuality, cancellations | Any symptom, condition, diagnosis or reason for attending |
| Admin — invoices, fees, rebates, Medicare, NDIS, paperwork, reports | Any treatment, therapeutic technique, medication, assessment or test result |
| Premises, parking, accessibility, video and telehealth quality | Any statement about outcome, progress, improvement, recovery or effectiveness |
| Manner and communication — courtesy, clarity, felt listened to | Any clinical recommendation, such as suggesting you for a particular problem |
| Practical logistics — languages spoken, appointment times, location | Any claim about your clinical skill or expertise |

<Note>
  This cuts both ways: a review can't say therapy helped, and it can't say it didn't. Outcome claims are
  out regardless of whether they flatter you.
</Note>

## Screening

Nothing reaches a profile until it has been screened, in two stages: a word list, then a model that reads the review against the boundary above.

A review is published only when both stages agree it's clean. Every other path leaves it unpublished — a clinical verdict, an unclear verdict, a word-list hit the model didn't see, a malformed response, an unreachable model, screening not being configured at all. **There is no path that publishes on a timeout.** A review sitting in a queue costs one person a day's wait; a wrongly published one is an offence.

Three outcomes:

| Outcome | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Approved** | Published on your profile. The author is told *"Thanks — your review is live on the profile."* |
| **Rejected** | Not stored at all, because there's nothing left to decide. The author is told which kind of content the rules stop us showing, and can write a different one |
| **Escalated** | Held for a person to read. The author is told *"Thanks — your review is with our team and will appear once it's been read."* |

The reason a review is held or rejected is recorded as one or more of: mentions a symptom, condition or diagnosis; describes treatment, technique or medication; describes a clinical outcome or improvement; recommends the practitioner for a clinical need.

Implication counts. *"I can finally sleep through the night"* is an outcome even though it names no diagnosis, and the model is instructed to read it that way.

The word list is deliberately blunt and deliberately not the decider. It catches roughly one clinical mention in ten on the reviews this directory already carries, because clinical content is a matter of meaning rather than vocabulary. A hit on its own never publishes and never rejects — it sends the review to a person, since terms that broad also appear in perfectly publishable sentences.

## What you can't do

<Warning>
  You have **no moderation, reply or takedown control** over reviews. You can't remove one, respond
  publicly, or ask for one to be reconsidered because you disagree with it.
</Warning>

Screening is Open Therapy's responsibility and is applied identically for everyone. Leaving it with you would make it your advertising decision, which is exactly the thing the National Law is about.

Reviews also don't appear anywhere in your workspace. When one is published you're told — *"You've got a new review"*, with the star rating — and the link takes you to your public profile, because that's the only place it's legible.

## What you can do

Write to [support@opentherapy.app](mailto:support@opentherapy.app) if a published review names a diagnosis, describes treatment, or contains anything else that shouldn't have cleared screening. A review published in breach is our problem to fix, not yours to absorb. Use the same address if a review is abusive or identifies a third party.

## Don't solicit reviews

<Warning>
  Asking clients for testimonials about your clinical care can itself breach the s133 advertising
  requirements, independently of anything Open Therapy publishes. Don't ask clients to review your
  treatment, and don't offer anything in exchange for a review.
</Warning>

A written review can run to 2,000 characters, and a rating is one to five stars.

## Your rating

Your star rating and review count are recomputed from your approved reviews each time one is published. They feed **Highest rated**, one of the three directory sort options alongside **Soonest available** and **Lowest price**. Two therapists with the same average are separated by the number of reviews behind it.

## Further reading

Ahpra publishes guidance on advertising a regulated health service, including the testimonial prohibition. Read it at [ahpra.gov.au](https://www.ahpra.gov.au) — it applies to your own website and social media as well as your Open Therapy profile.
